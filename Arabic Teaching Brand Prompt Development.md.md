## 1. Brand Foundation, Vision & Positioning

### 1.1 Tagline, Vision & Mission

* **Brand Name:** لِتَعَارَفُوا (*Li-ta'arafu* — *"so that you may know one another"*, Qur'an 49:13)
* **Tagline:** *To know one another.*
* **Vision:** A world where Arabic isn't locked behind one rigid classroom style or a single language—where anyone, anywhere, can learn it in the way their brain actually works and genuinely get to know one another.
* **Mission:** We create living Arabic classes, books, and learning tools built for different learning styles and translated into every language—bringing Fusha, everyday Egyptian speech, and the Qur'an together so learners can connect with real people.

### 1.2 Brand Story

The name comes from Surah al-Hujuraat (49:13):

> يَا أَيُّهَا النَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقْنَاكُم مِّن ذَكَرٍ وَأُنثَىٰ وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا
>
> *"O people, We created you from a male and a female, and made you nations and tribes so that you may know one another."*

The verse points straight at human difference and gives it a clear purpose: we are different so we can come to know one another. That idea is the heart of everything we build.

Language is how people actually get to know each other, yet Arabic is almost always taught in pieces. You learn one kind of Arabic in a classroom, hear a completely different one around the dinner table or on the street, and meet a third one when you open the Qur'an. Most schools treat those as three separate subjects. We treat them as one family growing from the same roots.

**Long-form story (for the About page):**
Every year, thousands of people put real hours into studying Arabic—in university lectures, weekend schools, and evening classes—only to land in Cairo and struggle to order a coffee. Thousands more grew up hearing Arabic in their grandmother's kitchen, yet can't make sense of the Qur'an they recite. And countless parents watch their kids' Arabic slip away a little more each school year.

They all run into the same wall: Arabic was handed to them in disconnected pieces. Fusha in one room, spoken dialect in another, the Qur'an in a third.

**لِتَعَارَفُوا** exists to knock that wall down. We teach Arabic the way it actually lives—following one root at a time from the page, to the street, to the Qur'an. When you realize that the word in a modern novel, the phrase your mother-in-law uses over dinner, and the verse you recite in prayer all grow out of the exact same three letters, Arabic stops feeling like three different languages. It becomes one living language—and it becomes yours.

We start with Egyptian Arabic because decades of cinema, music, and everyday storytelling have made it the most widely understood dialect across the Arab world. And from there, we are building books, classes, and learning tools designed for every kind of learner, in every language on earth.

*Li-ta'arafu. To know one another.*

### 1.3 Positioning Statement

For English-speaking learners who want Arabic for real human connection—heritage adults, partners, students, diaspora families, and Qur'an readers—**لِتَعَارَفُوا** is the Arabic learning home that teaches Fusha, spoken Egyptian Arabic, and Qur'anic vocabulary together through shared roots. Within about a year of focused learning, the three naturally "click" into one language you can read, speak, and feel at home in. Unlike apps and textbooks that wall off Fusha from everyday speech, we follow the living thread connecting them—so nothing you learn goes to waste and every single root opens three doors.

### 1.4 Core Pillars

1. **One Root, Three Worlds:** Every lesson starts with a three-letter root and traces how it lives in Fusha, on the Egyptian street, and in the Qur'an. *(Proof: Weekly newsletter with flashcards, courses, upcoming books)*
2. **Fusha, Made Cool:** No dusty grammar-museum vibes. We show Fusha where it actually breathes—in song lyrics, cinema, poetry, headlines, and witty everyday expressions. *(Proof: Culture-first lessons built around real media and street signs)*
3. **Connection First:** Grammar serves relationships, not the other way around. Every lesson helps you talk to a grandparent, laugh with your partner's family, chat with a shopkeeper, or feel present in prayer. *(Proof: Conversation-led cohorts and practical cultural notes)*
4. **Plain-English Qur'an:** High-frequency Qur'anic vocabulary explained clearly in everyday language—no heavy *tafseer* jargon, no watered-down meanings. *(Proof: Bite-sized Qur'anic vocabulary mini-courses)*
5. **Warm Rigour:** Relaxed and human, never sloppy. Every Arabic word includes full *tashkeel*, clear Egyptian Franco-Arab transliteration, and honest notes on how people actually use it. *(Proof: Strict bilingual typography and teacher QA)*

### 1.5 How Uniting Fusha and Dialect Makes Fusha "Cool"

Fusha only feels stiff when you never see it outside a textbook. The moment you spot it hiding inside everyday street speech, it flips from "homework" to the original source code everyone is remixing:

1. **Fusha is the source code; dialect is the remix.** When someone in Cairo says تِسْلَم إِيدَك (*tislam eedak*, "bless your hands"), they are riffing on the Fusha root **س - ل - م** with street rhythm. Hearing the deep root inside casual speech gives you an insider ear.
2. **Fusha is the language of style.** From Umm Kulthum's lyrics and Mahmoud Darwish's poetry to film titles, sharp brand slogans, and journalism, Fusha is heritage you still wear.
3. **Fusha is your passport across the region.** Dialects shift from country to country, while Fusha travels everywhere. Egyptian gives you warmth and intimacy; Fusha gives you reach.
4. **Designing for the "click" moment.** Around 6 to 12 months in, learners hit a moment where they hear a casual street phrase, immediately recognize its Fusha root, and connect it to a Qur'anic verse. We build every course toward that moment—and celebrate it when it happens.

### 2.2 Contrast Audit (WCAG 2.1)

* **Obsidian Ink (`#1B2028`) on Warm Papyrus (`#F7F3EB`):** `15.93 : 1` — Passes AAA for all text.
* **Royal Lapis Lazuli (`#1B3B6F`) on Warm Papyrus (`#F7F3EB`):** `10.05 : 1` — Passes AAA for all text (both directions).
* **Deep Carnelian (`#A33620`) on Warm Papyrus (`#F7F3EB`):** `6.12 : 1` — Passes AA comfortably for all small text, links, and dialect tags.
* **Malachite Green (`#2F664B`) on Warm Papyrus (`#F7F3EB`):** `6.08 : 1` — Passes AA comfortably for all body text and Qur'anic labels.
* **Carnelian Red-Orange (`#C84B31`) on Warm Papyrus (`#F7F3EB`):** `4.21 : 1` — Use for large headings (`>= 24px` or `>= 19px` bold), buttons with white text, and graphic accents.
* **Egyptian Turquoise (`#248EA6`) & Hammered Gold (`#C49A3A`):** Decorative and structural accents only (hairline dividers, borders, *nuqta* dots, icon highlights). Never use for small text on Warm Papyrus.
* **Never:** Place Carnelian or Gold text directly on Royal Lapis Lazuli.

### 2.3 Colour Usage & "Three Worlds" Coding

* **Proportion (60–25–10–5):** 60% Warm Papyrus backgrounds/cards (never pure `#FFFFFF`), 25% Obsidian Ink & Royal Lapis Lazuli, 10% Carnelian Red-Orange / Deep Carnelian, 5% Malachite Green, Egyptian Turquoise, and Hammered Gold trim.
* **Consistent "Three Worlds" Layer Coding:**
  * 🔵 **Fusha:** Royal Lapis Lazuli (`#1B3B6F`)
  * 🟠 **Egyptian / Street Dialect:** Carnelian Red-Orange (`#C84B31`) for large headers/accents; Deep Carnelian (`#A33620`) for small text/badges
  * 🟢 **Qur'anic Meaning:** Malachite Green (`#2F664B`)
* **Buttons & Dividers:** Primary CTA uses Royal Lapis Lazuli fill (`#1B3B6F`) with Warm Papyrus text (hover `#132B52`). Secondary community CTA uses Deep Carnelian fill (`#A33620`) or Carnelian Red-Orange (`#C84B31`) with bold `>= 18px` white text. Use `1px` Hammered Gold (`#C49A3A`) borders and dividers to frame root cards like *cloisonné* inlays.

### 2.4 Bilingual Typography & *Tashkeel* Rules

* **Arabic Font:** `Amiri` (Google Fonts, Naskh serif) — weights `400` and `700`.
* **English/Latin Font:** `Lora` (Google Fonts, editorial serif) — weights `400` and `600`, plus italic.
* **Optical Scaling:** Set Arabic at roughly `1.2x` the pixel size of adjacent English text so both scripts feel visually balanced.
* **Type Scale (Desktop / Mobile):**
  * **Display:** Lora `56 / 40px` (`1.15` lh) · Amiri `68 / 48px` (`1.6` lh)
  * **H1:** Lora `44 / 32px` (`1.2` lh) · Amiri `52 / 38px` (`1.7` lh)
  * **H2:** Lora `32 / 26px` (`1.25` lh) · Amiri `38 / 30px` (`1.8` lh)
  * **H3:** Lora `24 / 20px` (`1.3` lh) · Amiri `28 / 24px` (`1.9` lh)
  * **Body Large:** Lora `20 / 18px` (`1.6` lh) · Amiri `24 / 22px` (`2.0` lh)
  * **Body:** Lora `18 / 16px` (`1.65` lh) · Amiri `22 / 20px` (`2.0` lh)
  * **Caption / Transliteration:** Lora `14px` italic (`1.5` lh) · Amiri `18px` (`2.0` lh)
* **Critical *Tashkeel* & Shaping Guardrails:**
  1. Always give vowelled Arabic at least `line-height: 2` (`leading-loose` in Tailwind) so stacked marks like *shadda + kasra* never clip.
  2. **Never** put `overflow: hidden` on containers holding Arabic text.
  3. **Never** apply `letter-spacing` (`tracking`) to Arabic (`letter-spacing: 0` always).
  4. **Never** use italics or faux bold on Arabic; emphasize via weight (`700`) or palette color.
  5. Always wrap Arabic spans in `lang="ar" dir="rtl"`.

### 2.5 Transliteration Standard: Egyptian Franco-Arab

No academic symbols, under-dots, or macrons anywhere. Use clean, intuitive **Egyptian Franco-Arab** so learners can read and text the way Egyptians actually write online:

| Arabic Letter / Sound | Franco-Arab | Example | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ع** (*'ayn*) | `3` | `3arafa`, `ma3rafsh`, `inta 3aaref` | Standard across Egypt |
| **ء** (*hamza*) & Egyptian **ق** | `2` | `2ahwa` (coffee), `ra2mak` (your number), `2aslama` | Glottal stop in spoken Egyptian |
| **ق** (*qaf* in Fusha / Qur'an) | `q` | `bi-qalbin`, `qawlan` | Pronounced *qaf* in Fusha & recitation |
| **ح** (*ha*) | `7` | `7ubb`, `ra7eem` | Deep breathy *h* |
| **خ** (*kha*) | `kh` (or `5`) | `khayr`, `khabar` | Use `kh` in lessons for readability (`5` noted as chat variant) |
| **غ** (*ghayn*) | `gh` | `ghareeb`, `lugha` | Soft gargled *r* sound |
| **ش** (*shin*) | `sh` (or `4`) | `shukran`, `itsharrafna` | Use `sh` in lessons for readability (`4` noted as chat variant) |
| **ص / ض / ط / ظ** | `s` / `d` / `t` / `z` | `sabr`, `siyaam`, `ta3aaruf` | Plain letters—no under-dots |
| **Long vowels (ا / ي / و)** | `aa` / `ee` / `oo` (or `a` / `i` / `u`) | `kitaab`, `saleem`, `maktoob` | Double vowels show stress clearly without macrons |

*(Note: For the English brand name in logos and titles, keep the friendly spelling **Li-ta'arafu**.)*

## 3. Brand Voice & Persona Guide

### 3.1 Brand Persona

**"The sharp, warm Cairene friend who studied at Al-Azhar and also knows every classic film quote."**
They are relaxed, funny, deeply knowledgeable, and zero-ego. They can unpack a Qur'anic word over tea without making it feel like a lecture, and two minutes later teach you how to banter with a taxi driver in Cairo. They never talk down to you, never show off, and always make you feel like you belong in the room.

### 3.2 Tone Attributes

* **Warm, never sugary:** Encouraging and personal, like a voice note from a good friend—not fake corporate enthusiasm.
* **Plain-spoken, never dumbed down:** One clear idea at a time, skipping linguistic jargon while respecting your intelligence.
* **Culturally plugged-in, never gimmicky:** Grounded in real music, food, humor, and family life rather than tourist tropes.
* **Reverent with the Qur'an, welcoming to everyone:** Clear, accurate, and thoughtful with verses—never preachy, sectarian, or exclusive.

### 3.3 Do's & Don'ts

* **Do** lead with real human moments ("so you can catch the joke at your in-laws' table," not "master Form VI verbs").
* **Do** always include full *tashkeel* and Egyptian Franco-Arab transliteration on Arabic examples.
* **Do** call Egyptian Arabic "spoken Arabic" or "living Arabic"—never "slang" or "broken Arabic."
* **Don't** sound like a brochure, a rigid academic syllabus, or a gamified streak app.
* **Don't** promise "fluency in 30 days." Our honest promise is real, noticeable connection within about a year of steady learning.
* **Don't** use guilt with heritage learners or parents ("you should already know this").

### 3.4 Messaging Angles by Audience

1. **Heritage Adults:** *"Your Arabic isn't gone—it's just waiting for the right key."* (Warm, zero-shame reconnection with family and roots.)
2. **Partners & Egypt-Bound Learners:** *"Don't just sit at the table—be in on the joke."* (Practical Egyptian conversation, cultural fluency, and everyday confidence.)
3. **University & Academic Students (18–70):** *"Make your textbook Fusha actually work on the street."* (Rigorous academic Fusha bridged directly to lived Egyptian culture.)
4. **Diaspora Youth (8–18, written to parents):** *"Arabic your kids will actually want to speak."* (Stories, games, real family conversation, and visible progress without weekend-school boredom.)
5. **Qur'anic Vocabulary Learners:** *"Know what the words mean while you're reciting them."* (Simple, jargon-free breakdowns of high-frequency Qur'anic roots for anyone who can already read the script.)

### 4.2 Interactive "One Root, Three Worlds" + Flashcard & Lesson Preview

* **Section Header:** See how one root lives three lives—and preview what's inside every lesson.
* **Subheader:** Almost every Arabic word grows out of a three-letter root. Learn that root once, and you start hearing it in books, on Cairo streets, and in the Qur'an. Explore a root below, flip a sample flashcard, or peek at real lesson and homework material.

#### Root 1: ع - ر - ف (`3 - R - F`) · *knowing, recognizing*

* **Core Idea:** Recognizing someone or something, and becoming familiar with them.
* 🔵 **Fusha (Standard Arabic):**
  * **عَرَفَ / يَعْرِفُ** (`3arafa / ya3rifu`) — he knew, recognized / he knows
  * **مَعْرِفَة** (`ma3rifa`) — knowledge, acquaintance
  * **تَعَارَفَ** (`ta3aarafa`) — to get to know one another
  * **تَعَارُف** (`ta3aaruf`) — getting acquainted, introductions
  * **اِعْتَرَفَ** (`i3tarafa`) — to admit, acknowledge
  * **عُرْف** (`3urf`) — custom, what a community recognizes as normal
  * *Pattern note:* Adding the *ta-aa* shape (**تَفَاعَلَ** · `tafaa3ala`) turns an action into something two people do together. **تَعَارَفَ** literally means *knowing each other*—that's where our name comes from.
* 🟠 **Egyptian Arabic (On the Street):**
  * **عِرِفْت** (`3irift`) — I found out / I knew
  * **إِنْتَ عَارِف…؟** (`inta 3aaref…?`) — Do you know…? *(use `inti 3arfa` when talking to a woman)*
  * **مَعْرَفْش** (`ma3rafsh`) — I don't know *(easily one of the top five phrases you'll hear in Egypt)*
  * **اِتْعَرَّفْنَا** (`it3arrafna`) — We got to know each other / we met
  * **دَه مَعْرِفَة** (`da ma3rifa`) — He's someone I know / an acquaintance
  * *How it shifts:* Fusha `3arafa` softens into `3irif` in Cairo. The short vowels move around, but the three root consonants (`3 - R - F`) never budge.
* 🟢 **Simple Qur'anic Meaning:**
  * **لِتَعَارَفُوا** (`li-ta3aarafoo`, Qur'an 49:13): *"so that you may know one another."* Human diversity isn't framed as a barrier—it's an invitation to get to know each other.
  * **الْمَعْرُوف** (`al-ma3roof`, e.g., 3:104): Literally *"what is recognized"*—the kind of decency and goodness any human heart naturally recognizes as right.
  * *In one line:* In the Qur'an, knowing is never just trivia—it's about knowing people and recognizing what is good.

#### Root 2: ك - ت - ب (`K - T - B`) · *writing*

* **Core Idea:** Writing something down, and by extension what is recorded, settled, or meant to be.
* 🔵 **Fusha (Standard Arabic):**
  * **كَتَبَ / يَكْتُبُ** (`kataba / yaktubu`) — he wrote / he writes
  * **كِتَاب** (`kitaab`) — book
  * **كَاتِب** (`kaatib`) — writer, author
  * **مَكْتَب** (`maktab`) — desk, office *(the place where writing happens)*
  * **مَكْتَبَة** (`maktaba`) — library, bookshop
  * **مَكْتُوب** (`maktoob`) — written; a letter
  * *Pattern note:* Put `ma-` in front of a root and you get the place where that action happens. **مَكْتَب** (`maktab`) is where you write—just like **مَدْرَسَة** (`madrasa`) is where you study (**د - ر - س**).
* 🟠 **Egyptian Arabic (On the Street):**
  * **بِيِكْتِب** (`biyiktib`) — he's writing / he writes
  * **اِكْتِبْ لِي رَقْمَك** (`iktib-li ra2mak`) — Write down your number for me
  * **مَكْتُوب** (`maktoob`) — It was meant to be *(literally "it's written"—said about fate, love, and life's twists)*
  * **كَتْب الْكِتَاب** (`katb il-kitaab`) — The marriage-contract ceremony *(literally "writing the book")*
  * **الْمَكْتَبَة** (`il-maktaba`) — The bookshop / stationery store
  * *Culture note:* In Egypt, `katb il-kitaab` is one of the happiest celebrations in a family's life. A root about putting pen to paper became the everyday phrase for a lifelong commitment.
* 🟢 **Simple Qur'anic Meaning:**
  * **ذَٰلِكَ الْكِتَابُ لَا رَيْبَ فِيهِ** (`dhaalika l-kitaabu laa rayba feeh`, 2:2): *"This is the Book, about which there is no doubt."* Throughout the text, the Qur'an refers to itself simply as **الْكِتَاب** (`al-Kitaab` — The Book).
  * **كُتِبَ عَلَيْكُمُ الصِّيَامُ** (`kutiba 3alaykumu s-siyaam`, 2:183): *"Fasting has been prescribed for you"*—literally *"written upon you."*
  * *In one line:* When the Qur'an says something is "written," it means it is settled and binding—which echoes right back in the Egyptian expression `maktoob`.

#### Root 3: س - ل - م (`S - L - M`) · *safety, peace, wholeness*

* **Core Idea:** Being safe, whole, and unharmed—and passing that peace on to someone else.
* 🔵 **Fusha (Standard Arabic):**
  * **سَلِمَ** (`salima`) — to be safe, sound, unharmed
  * **سَلَام** (`salaam`) — peace; greeting
  * **سَلِيم** (`saleem`) — sound, whole, healthy
  * **سَلَّمَ** (`sallama`) — to greet; to hand over safely
  * **أَسْلَمَ** (`2aslama`) — to entrust or submit oneself wholly
  * **إِسْلَام / مُسْلِم** (`2islaam / muslim`) — wholehearted peace/submission / one who lives in it
  * *Pattern note:* Doubling the middle letter (`sallama`) often means causing that state for someone else: making someone feel safe → greeting them warmly.
* 🟠 **Egyptian Arabic (On the Street):**
  * **سَلَامُو عَلِيكُو** (`salaamu 3aleeku`) — Hello *(everyday Cairo pronunciation)*
  * **مَعَ السَّلَامَة** (`ma3a s-salaama`) — Goodbye *(literally "go with safety")*
  * **سَلَام!** (`salaam!`) — Bye! *(quick and casual)*
  * **تِسْلَم إِيدَك** (`tislam eedak`) — Thank you / bless your hands *(said when someone cooks, makes tea, or helps you; `eedik` to a woman)*
  * **سَلِّم لِي عَلَى مَامَا** (`sallim-li 3ala maama`) — Say hi to your mom for me
  * **سَلَامْتَك** (`salamtak`) — Get well soon / hope you're okay *(literally "your safety"; `salamtik` to a woman)*
  * *Culture note:* Egyptians weave this root into conversation dozens of times a day. Every goodbye, thank-you, and get-well wish is literally wishing the other person safety.
* 🟢 **Simple Qur'anic Meaning:**
  * **السَّلَامُ** (`as-Salaam`, 59:23): One of God's names—The Source of Peace, completely free of any flaw or harm.
  * **بِقَلْبٍ سَلِيمٍ** (`bi-qalbin saleem`, 26:89): Coming to God *"with a sound heart"*—a heart that is whole, clear, and uncorrupted.
  * **سَلَامٌ قَوْلًا مِّن رَّبٍّ رَّحِيمٍ** (`salaamun qawlan min rabbin ra7eem`, 36:58): *"Peace—a word from a Merciful Lord."*
  * *In one line:* In the Qur'an, `salaam` goes far deeper than "hello"—it means wholeness and safety, and every street greeting in Cairo carries a quiet echo of it.

Your very first root is the one hidden right inside our name:

**ع - ر - ف** (`3 - R - F`) · *to know, to recognize*

🔵 **In Fusha**
**عَرَفَ** (`3arafa`) means "he knew" or "he recognized." Put it into the "doing something with each other" pattern and you get **تَعَارَفَ** (`ta3aarafa`): *to get to know one another*. You'll also see **مَعْرِفَة** (`ma3rifa`), meaning knowledge or acquaintance.

🟠 **On the Street in Egypt**
Walk through Cairo for ten minutes and you'll hear this root everywhere:
* **مَعْرَفْش** (`ma3rafsh`) — *"I don't know."* Honestly, the single most useful word in the country.
* **إِنْتَ عَارِف؟** (`inta 3aaref?`) — *"Do you know?"*
* **اِتْعَرَّفْنَا** (`it3arrafna`) — *"We've met / we got to know each other."* Usually answered with **اِتْشَرَّفْنَا** (`itsharrafna`), *"it's an honor."*

Notice how Fusha `3arafa` becomes `3irif` in Egyptian speech. The vowels shift, the rhythm relaxes, and the three root letters stay right where they are.

🟢 **In the Qur'an**
In Surah al-Hujuraat (49:13), God says:
وَجَعَلْنَاكُمْ شُعُوبًا وَقَبَائِلَ لِتَعَارَفُوا
*"…and We made you nations and tribes so that you may know one another."*
That's our tagline, our name, and the whole reason we exist.
